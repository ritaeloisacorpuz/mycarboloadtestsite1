Crave Cacao and the Roots of the Philippine Chocolate Industry

Bars and chips of chocolate have been part of the sugary palette of Filipinos and non-Filipinos for decades. They are often consumed after one’s main course meal is over, but with reasonable dietary limits to prevent health complications—notably diabetes—from occurring. Consumers, at least once in their lives, have encountered purchasing and ingesting these confectionery products out of pleasure. Meanwhile, others prefer to offer these chocolate bars and cookies as a sign of appreciation and love for colleagues, family members, and friends. Products like the Philippines’ very own Ricoa Flat Tops (manufactured by Comfoods) and Jack & Jill Nips (owned by Universal Robina Corporation, a subsidiary under the John Gokongwei Jr. Group); the United States’ Hershey’s, M&M’s, and Reese’s; Japan’s Meiji and Glico (the producer of Pocky flavor-coated sticks); and Switzerland’s Lindt dominate the cocoa landscape of the planet. These companies regularly rake in millions in revenue generated from consumer sales in various countries.

The post-World War II era, apparently, was the renaissance of Japanese cuisine in the Philippines. This was where the advent of beloved Japanese restaurants or food chains like Tokyo Tokyo, Ramen Nagi, Coco Ichibanya, Santouka, and Botejyu happened in the Philippine market. Because of the palatable selections being offered, Filipinos instantly crave the flavors incorporated in Japanese dishes, such as the ones previously enumerated. Established in 1967 right at the heart of Quezon City’s home of the “Big Dome,” the Araneta Center (now the modern-day Araneta City since 2019), Matsuzaka House brought authentic Japanese cuisine to the Filipino mass market. In Philippine restaurant aficionado circles, this particular establishment trailblazed or pioneered the modern Japanese dining experience in the archipelago. After this historic moment, succeeding Japanese restaurants were constructed in the Philippines, aiming to continue this casual yet heritage-centered Asian dining experience. For instance, Tokyo Tokyo made its debut in April 1985 right at Glorietta (which was then coined as the Quad Car Park). Unlike premium Japanese restaurants, this popular chain aims to employ a fast-paced food service system to serve delicious dishes to Filipinos with varying wallet sizes. Tokyo Tokyo branches do employ traditional Japanese motifs within their surroundings, making them conducive for casual diners to be immersed in the East Asian nation’s rich cultural and culinary heritage without compromising their monthly or weekly budgets.

Tong Yang is a buffet-type restaurant that offers dishes that predominantly focus on Asian cuisine. This is in stark contrast to Vikings, which specializes in a wide variety of dishes across the globe. Actually, this was where some of my extended family members celebrated the diamond birthday milestone (i.e., 75th birthday) of my maternal grandmother. The photo above prominently features the classic and iconic California Maki variant of the world-famous sushi. It contains a mango piece, which is wrapped with standard white rice, rice vinegar, crab sticks (i.e., kani), and cucumber. When consumed in just a bite, this orange-themed sushi gives out a sweet and savory taste of the mango component. This makes a diner supplement his or her dietary requirements while enjoying his or her dining experience. Other dishes like tempura and miso soup, accompanied by vegetable and meat side dishes, were also offered by the hospitable buffet crew.
